# Permanent Capital Index™ — Theoretical Framework

**Version 4.0 — June 2026**
**Companion to the [Index Methodology](pci-index-methodology.md). The methodology defines
*how* constituents are selected; this document explains *why* the category is expected to
have an edge. Where the two conflict, the methodology governs.**

---

## 1. The thesis in one paragraph

A small, globally dispersed class of public companies shares a single piece of
organizational DNA: their primary activity is **owning and reallocating capital across a
diversified set of assets**, financed by capital that has **no redemption claim and an
indefinite horizon**. We call them permanent capital allocators. The claim of this index
is that two *structural* properties — permanence of capital and owner alignment — give the
category a durable behavioral and governance advantage over the broad market and over
conventional active managers. The index is built to *test* that claim honestly, by
selecting on the structure rather than on past returns.

---

## 2. Why structure, not performance, defines membership

It is tempting to define "great allocators" as "the ones that compounded fastest." That
definition is self-refuting for an index: if membership requires having outperformed, the
index cannot then serve as evidence that the category outperforms — the conclusion is
assumed in the selection, and every backtest is contaminated by look-ahead and
survivorship.

This framework grounds the index in **forward-stable structural attributes** and treats
performance strictly as an *output*. If the category has a real edge, a structurally
selected index will show it. If not, that is an honest and interesting finding in its own
right. Neither outcome requires curating winners after the fact.

---

## 3. The two pillars of the edge

### 3.1 Permanence of capital → no forced selling

The deepest disadvantage of most pooled capital is that it can be withdrawn. Open-end
funds face redemptions; closed-end and LP vehicles face finite lives and gates; operating
companies face refinancing walls. Withdrawal risk forces selling into weakness — exactly
when an allocator should be buying.

A permanent capital allocator is funded by capital that cannot be redeemed: permanent
equity, retained earnings, or insurance float. This is the structural precondition for the
most-quoted idea in value investing — *be greedy when others are fearful* — to be
**operationally possible** rather than merely aspirational. The edge is not cleverness; it
is the *absence of a structural handicap* that afflicts almost everyone else.

### 3.2 Owner alignment → low agency cost

Concentrated, long-tenured ownership — a founding family, a foundation, a controlling
holding company, a long-serving owner-operator — aligns the decision-maker's horizon with
multi-decade compounding rather than quarterly optics, index-hugging, or fee maximization.
Skin in the game replaces career risk as the dominant incentive. This is why so many of
the category's exemplars are family- or foundation-controlled: the structure selects for
patience.

### 3.3 Why these two, and not "diversification"

Diversified allocation is the *activity* that defines the category, and the concept requires
it (no single-asset or mono-line operators). Free data cannot measure it reliably, so the
methodology discloses it rather than enforcing it as a filter. But breadth is not the edge — plenty of
diversified conglomerates destroy value. The edge is the combination of *permanent capital*
(so the allocator can act counter-cyclically) and *aligned ownership* (so it allocates well
rather than empire-builds).

## 4. The archetypes: the structural forms of the category

Permanent capital allocators do not all look alike. The phenomenon takes several distinct
structural forms worldwide. The exemplars below illustrate each form; membership itself is
decided by the mechanical rule in the methodology, which admits every company that passes it.

**Float allocators — insurance float deployed into ownership.**
The canonical form. Premiums collected today and paid later are a pool of capital with
negative cost if underwriting is profitable — and no redemption claim. What qualifies a
company is not earning premiums but *allocating that float into equities and whole
businesses with investment as a core competency*. **Berkshire Hathaway, Fairfax, and
Markel** do this. Large diversified or life insurers — **Allianz, AXA**, and most monoline
underwriters — do not: they match liabilities with fixed income and earn an
underwriting/spread margin. The index does **not** hand-exclude them, however: membership
is decided by a single mechanical allocation test (does the balance sheet show an
investment-dominated structure?), not by a curator. Whether a given insurer qualifies is
an empirical question the rule answers, and if the inclusive result behaves like a
sector fund, the criteria are revisited, never the individual names.

**Portfolio-manager allocators — permanent balance-sheet capital alongside management.**
Firms that invest their own permanent capital into real assets and businesses, not merely
collect fees on others' money. **Brookfield** is the exemplar: it owns infrastructure, real
estate, and operating businesses on its own balance sheet with an indefinite horizon. Pure
fee-only asset gatherers, whose capital is their clients' and is redeemable, are a
different animal and are excluded.

**Industrial aggregators — diversified ownership and capital rotation.**
The Japanese *sogo shosha* (**Mitsubishi Corp, Itochu, Mitsui, Marubeni, Sumitomo**).
Post-war, these were pure trading intermediaries; over sixty years they accumulated equity
stakes in resources, infrastructure, and operating companies across every continent,
turning their trading network into an information engine that feeds capital allocation —
structurally analogous to how Berkshire's insurance operations feed its. Their balance
sheets are dominated by long-term investments and associates, not by factories. Excluding
them would be a Western-centric bias: where the *zaibatsu* were dismantled and family
holding companies never formed, the *sogo shosha* is Japan's permanent capital vehicle —
funded by bank relationships rather than float because Japanese capital markets developed
differently.

**Family / permanent holding companies — aligned, diversified, perpetual.**
Publicly traded corporations whose business is owning diversified stakes, funded by patient
family or foundation capital with no redemption mechanism. **Investor AB** (Wallenberg),
**Ackermans & van Haaren**, **HAL Trust**, **Industrivarden**, and dozens of lesser-known
vehicles worldwide. Conceptually, single-asset holding companies do not belong; the defining
feature is diversified, perpetual, aligned ownership, although diversification and alignment
are disclosed rather than enforced by the mechanical rule.

| Archetype | Illustrative examples | What the allocation test looks for |
|-----------|------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Float allocator | Berkshire, Fairfax, Markel | float deployed into ownership, not just liability-matched bonds |
| Portfolio-manager allocator | Brookfield | assets owned with permanent balance-sheet capital |
| Industrial aggregator | Mitsubishi, Itochu, Marubeni | diversified investments & associates dominate the balance sheet |
| Family / permanent holding | Investor AB, Industrivarden, 3i | diversified, perpetual equity stakes |

*(These are motivating exemplars. Membership is decided mechanically by the rule in the
methodology, not by this table.)*

## 5. What the index does and does not claim

**It claims:** to be a reproducible, structurally defined, openly specified
benchmark for the permanent-capital category — and to test, without circularity, whether
permanence and alignment confer a durable advantage.

**It does not claim:** guaranteed outperformance. The theoretical edge is plausible but
conditional and regime-dependent; permanent capital tends to lag in momentum-driven,
mega-cap-led melt-ups and to shine in dislocations and through full cycles. Reported
performance is subject to the limitations in the methodology (7), including survivorship
bias that free data cannot fully remove. The honesty of the framing is the point.

---

## 6. Why this category is overlooked (the opportunity)

Most investors know Berkshire and almost none know Sofina, Sabvest, or Soul Pattinson —
despite long track records. The category is invisible because it is scattered
across small and mid-cap names in two dozen countries, hidden under heterogeneous accounting
labels and unglamorous "holding company" classifications, and absent from the cap-weighted
indices that dominate attention. Surfacing it — including the names no one has heard of — is
both the intellectual contribution and the reason a bottom-up, market-wide screen (rather
than a hand-picked list of the usual suspects) matters.
